#31199. 4 steps for running a panel people actually want to attend

Suppose you are called upon to organize a panel discussion. The task may look easy. But if you’re not careful, you might end up overseeing a boring multispeaker event or a free-for-all where panelists interrupt each other and talk at cross-purposes.

It is up to you to make this format work and create a coherent, engaging session. But how? Follow the steps below and you’ll bring order to the panel, inspire listeners, and create an exciting event.

1. Choose Speakers

Your first task is to select the speakers. Choose people who are knowledgeable and bring different perspectives.

Suppose your topic is “AI in our company.” You might select panelists who show how artificial intelligence is being used creatively in a variety of disciplines in the company. One speaker might discuss how AI is improving the hiring process. Another might discuss how AI is helping leaders. To make things interesting, include a naysayer, someone who says AI is a hazard to achieving the company’s goals.

2. Brief the Speakers

Let the presenters know why they’ve been chosen and  what they should focus on. Tell them who the other speakers are and what perspective they might bring.

Suppose the topic is “What Makes a Good Leader.” Suggest that each speaker focuses on a quality they feel is critical to effective leadership, such as charisma or emotional intelligence. Make sure your request is a good fit for each of them.

Tell presenters how long they will have to speak and if there will be an audience Q&A. That will allow them to prepare appropriate talking points. Make clear that the time limits are more than suggestions. They are needed so the event goes smoothly.

3. Moderate the Event

Your role will likely include moderating the event.

Open the session with a brief overview of the topic, its importance, and the range of perspectives that will be covered. Your opening will help listeners better understand the discussion—and why this panel is important.

Introduce each speaker. It’s best to do this when that individual is about to present, rather than introducing everyone at the start. In your introductions you can suggest how a person’s presentation relates to the larger themes. That underscores the coherence of the overall panel. You want to avoid the disaster of disconnected speeches.

When the last speaker has concluded and you have thanked all the panelists and provided a brief summary, invite questions from the audience if the format of your event permits. This is an exciting part of the event, so save plenty of time for Q&A.

It is likely that the questions will be targeted to particular speakers. If not, you choose the best candidate to answer that question.

Always prepare some questions yourself. That’s particularly useful if there are few from the audience or if you feel an important area has been neglected.

Throughout the panel discussion keep the tone enjoyable. The best events are lively and full of fun and laughter.

4. Conclude the Event

Conclude by summing up what the audience can take away from the panel and what the overall message is.

Thank the audience for coming and conclude with a call to action that suggests how the audience can act upon the knowledge it has gained.

While research from Pew finds that most people like their bosses, about one in five give their direct managers low ratings—and that can have a significant impact. Gallup reports that only 30% of American workers are engaged in their workplace and that managers account for at least 70% of the variance between motivated and unmotivated employees.

Most are thrust into the role, with very little training or development, because they were good at something else or there was simply a hole to be filled.

The truth is that most leaders fail because they don’t understand how to do their job. They get bad advice or try to emulate “tough” bosses like the ones they see in movies like Steve Jobs or The Devil Wears Prada, giving them misconceptions about what it takes to be effective. You may think you’re a good boss, but if you’re doing one of these things, you’re probably not. 

1. View the employment relationship as a transaction rather than a values-based relationship

Many managers take an economist’s view of the workplace. Employees do work for you. You pay them. Neither side is acting out of charity, but in your own self-interest. It is much as Adam Smith wrote in 1776’s The Wealth of Nations: “It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity but to their self-love, and never talk to them of our own necessities but of their advantages.”

Yet that quote, while accurate, is misconstrued. Smith was making an observation about how market forces help coordinate activity among strangers. He certainly wasn’t making a recommendation about how we conduct our business and personal relationships. In fact, in his other great work, The Theory of Moral Sentiments, he argued quite passionately for the importance of empathy (which he referred to as sympathy). 

More importantly, since Smith’s time, we have accumulated centuries of evidence that acting out of pure self-interest can be problematic. Consider the “prisoner’s dilemma,” a famous thought experiment in which individuals acting in their self-interest make everyone worse off. In a wide array of real-world and experimental contexts, people will cooperate for the greater good rather than pursue pure self-interest.

As organizational psychologist and workplace expert David Burkus explained in his 2022 TED Talk, humans are prosocial. We’re more likely to perform when we understand and identify who benefits from our work than when we are driven by financial incentives alone. Evolutionary psychologists have long established that altruism is deeply embedded in our sense of tribe.

Burkus references a study by Adam Grant in which the performance of call-center employees more than doubled when they had regular conversations with people who benefited from their work. Lisa Earle McLeod and Elizabeth Lotardo report in an article in Harvard Business Review that similar results have been found in studies of lifeguards, hospital workers, and sales teams.

2. Focus too much on incentives, rather than norms and rituals

A leader’s most important job is to shape behavior, and most start with incentives. Design the right mix of carrots and sticks, so the thinking goes. Yet human behavior can’t be so easily boiled down to simple triggers. Decades of research show that incentives can often be problematic. They often fail and can even backfire horrendously.

Consider the “ultimatum game,” a popular social science experiment in which one player is given a dollar and asked to propose how it should be divided with another player. If the offer is accepted, both players receive the agreed-upon shares. If it is not accepted, neither player gets anything. If people responded perfectly to incentives, the second player would accept even a single penny. After all, a penny is better than nothing.

But they don’t. In fact, the ultimatum game and related experiments—such as the “dictator game,” the “public goods game,” and the “third-party punishment game”—reveal that human behavior is incredibly complex. These have been repeated countless times in different contexts and they show that people regularly act against their interests to express what they think is fair and right. In the ultimatum game, offers below roughly 30% are routinely rejected.

What is often missed is that there are norms and rituals that underlie behaviors. This helps explain why people from different cultures behave differently in the ultimatum game and its variants, and that’s what most incentive efforts miss. You can’t separate punishments and rewards from the cultural context. Often, there are underlying motivations that extrinsic rewards and punishments fail to account for.

Incentives don’t operate in a vacuum. You can’t motivate people effectively without understanding the culture that shapes what motivates them.

3. Treat talent as innate, rather than something you develop

In 1997, McKinsey published a landmark article declaring a “war for talent.” The firm argued that due to demographic shifts, recruiting the “best and the brightest” was even more important than “capital, strategy, or R&D.” The report was enormously influential and continues to affect how leaders manage their teams even today.

I once worked at a company where senior leadership meetings on Friday mornings were meant to discuss critical issues. But no matter the agenda, the conversation always seemed to turn back to talent and the need for “better people.” They would look at our current staff and wish that they could find others who were smarter, more skilled, and more ambitious.

Each time, I remember thinking, “You recruited these people. You trained these people. And you manage these people. If there’s a talent problem, it doesn’t lie with them. It lies with you.” As Burkus puts it: “Talent doesn’t make the team. The team makes the talent.” Their people weren’t failing them; they were failing their people, which is why our employee turnover rate was roughly twice the industry average.

The truth is that we don’t need the best people; we need the best teams. Researchers at MIT and Carnegie Mellon found that group performance is driven more by factors such as group dynamics and social sensitivity than anything else. It’s how your team builds trust, psychological safety, and collaboration that will determine what they can achieve.

If you feel you need “better people,“ you should probably focus your efforts on becoming a more capable leader and creating a better, more supportive culture that empowers people to achieve their potential.

When Alfred Sloan created the modern corporation at General Motors, he based it on the military. The company was split into divisions, each with its own leadership. Information flowed up, orders went down, and your rank determined your responsibility. The model assumed that leaders had a better understanding than those on the lower rungs. Managers made plans and foot soldiers carried them out.

That model made sense in an industrial economy. In the 20th century, more than 80% of U.S. corporate assets were tangible assets, or things like factories, equipment, and real estate. Good leaders were supposed to be wise, but firm taskmasters.

Today, however, the script has flipped and more than 80% of corporate assets are intangible, such as licenses, patents, and other intellectual property. The primary purpose of today’s leaders is to manage ecosystems of talent, technology, and information. There is simply no way for a single person to have enough visibility to control all of that. 

The role of a leader has changed. It is no longer enough merely to plan and direct action. You need to inspire meaning and empower belief. The truth is that the lunatics really do run the asylum. As a leader, it’s your job to help them run it better.

#31214. What we learned from 250,000 public workers about AI adoption

As generative AI sweeps across industries, some workers using it are reporting increases in productivity. But inside government, where those benefits could translate into better public services, many employees are still unsure how the technology applies to their jobs, how to use it, or whether they are allowed to.

That’s one takeaway from our research at InnovateUS, a nonprofit learning initiative that has trained more than 250,000 government workers in AI, digital technology, and innovative skills. They work in all 50 states and the District of Columbia, in jobs ranging from public safety and benefits administration to legal services and executive leadership. Our aim is to give people working in state and local government accessible opportunities to develop skills that help them serve the public, and ultimately deliver services worthy of residents’ trust. 

During no-cost skill-building workshops across the country and on the web, public servants trade experiences about what works and what doesn’t. Participants have explored using artificial intelligence to improve benefits delivery with New York City’s health commissioner and learned from current and former state-government leaders how to introduce workers to using and even building AI. Recently, one state leader in Colorado described how he is using generative AI to improve performance reviews; he reported better feedback, greater worker satisfaction, reduced turnover, and time savings. These exchanges help public professionals learn from one another’s experiments and consider what they could adapt to their own agencies.

Learning together online also gives us an opportunity to listen systematically to people’s experiences with AI. That listening underpins the first report from our Observatory of Public Sector AI, drawing on 106,300 precourse survey responses and 67,002 postcourse responses from participants in our introductory generative AI course, collected between June 2024 and May 2026. The findings reveal a substantial gap between the headlines around generative AI and everyday working life in government.

What keeps public workers from using AI

Among public-sector respondents entering our foundational generative AI course, 51% had never tried the technology. Another 14% had experimented with it personally but weren’t yet using it at work. While technology companies race to build autonomous agents, many workers are still trying to understand what AI has to do with their jobs, why they are being asked to use it, and how to use it well. They’re also not sure if they’re allowed to use it at all.

The findings complicate the stereotype of government employees as resistant to change. Among respondents who weren’t using generative AI, 28% said they didn’t think it applied to their work, 23% didn’t know how to use it, and 19% didn’t know whether they were allowed to use it at work.

Managers face similar uncertainty. Among managers who weren’t using AI, 24% didn’t know how, and 22% didn’t know whether it was permitted. Employees look to supervisors for guidance, but those supervisors may need help themselves.

Listening to the workforce gives leaders something concrete to address. Providing access to a tool or publishing a policy leaves substantial work undone. Employees need relevant examples, opportunities to practice, and managers prepared to support responsible use.

To turn AI use into public value, we need to help employees connect new skills to specific service problems. This might look like reducing wait times when people call about health or unemployment insurance, using AI to make it cheaper and easier to engage the public in policymaking, or even knowing how to regulate and buy AI to reduce dependence on any one vendor. And we need to give them the support to change how work gets done. Systematic workforce training creates an opportunity to build that capability to listen, learn and deliver, and to understand whether it makes a difference. 

Beginners report the largest gains

One of our most encouraging findings concerns people starting with the least familiarity. In the matched responses, learners who entered with no reported AI knowledge showed the largest gains in reported knowledge. Those who began with no confidence also experienced particularly substantial improvements in confidence.

This parallels research in the private sector. A study of private sector customer-support workers by academics at Stanford and MIT found that less experienced and lower-skilled workers gained the most from an AI assistant. That study measured workplace performance; ours measures reported learning. Together, these studies suggest that organizations should pay attention to people who are just getting started. Concentrating support on enthusiastic early adopters risks overlooking workers who could benefit considerably from an introduction.

We also found a striking relationship between confidence and the possibilities people could envision for generative AI tools. After training, highly confident respondents planned to apply AI to an average of 3.8 tasks, compared with 1.5 among those with low confidence. That association does not establish that confidence causes broader use. It suggests that learning involves more than acquiring facts about a technology. People also need to feel capable of identifying and trying appropriate applications.

Confidence alone, however, is an incomplete measure of success. Someone can feel more capable without becoming more accurate. Training must develop the judgment to question an answer, verify a source, protect sensitive information, and recognize when AI is inappropriate. We need to assess those capabilities alongside confidence.

From better tasks to better services

When asked how they hoped to use AI, participants readily described activities such as drafting reports, creating presentations, and summarizing documents. Fewer responses connected those activities to outcomes such as making services more accessible or improving public participation.

Our questions partly explain that pattern. We asked about specific tasks, and brief open-ended responses offered limited insight into what people hoped to accomplish through them. We have begun collecting voice-based feedback and  are adding questions that probe whether learning is helping employees improve services.

Private-sector research underscores why the distinction between individual learning and organizational outcomes matters. In McKinsey’s 2026 global survey, 80% of respondents said AI improved their own productivity, yet only 37% reported a positive impact on their organization’s earnings. Helping individuals work faster does not automatically improve an organization’s performance.

For democratic institutions, the corresponding test is whether time saved translates into better public services, more engagement with residents, and more effective problem solving. Does faster document processing reduce the wait for benefits? Does clearer communication help more people complete an application? Does using AI to analyze public feedback lead to a better decision? Ultimately, do people trust government more? Training needs to help employees make those connections, and evaluation needs to tell us whether they hold up in practice.

What we still need to learn

Our first report captures an early stage in a much longer process. Its surveys are voluntary, and participants are a large but not representative sample of the government workforce. The results measure reported knowledge, confidence, and intended use immediately before and after a course. They do not establish whether people retain what they learn, apply it consistently, make fewer mistakes, or deliver better services. Without a comparison group, we also cannot isolate the effect of training from other influences. The scale of the dataset helps us examine patterns across professions and organizations, though it does not remove those limitations. 

Our opportunities to learn more are accelerating. As our learner population grows and we expand the range of people teaching and learning from one another, we can investigate which approaches help different workers and what support they need after an introductory course.

Places such as Washington, D.C., which has adopted a whole-of-workforce training approach, offer an especially valuable opportunity. Studying learning across an organization can help us understand how individual skills interact with managerial support, access to tools, and agency rules.

The most exciting next step is connecting evidence about learning with administrative measures of service performance and other data on trust in government. With appropriate privacy protections, we could examine whether training is associated with shorter wait times, more accurate case resolution, fewer application errors, easier access to services, and more outreach to residents 

Those studies will require careful design. A shorter queue could reflect staffing changes, new software, or lower demand. Following teams over time and comparing their experiences could help us understand whether, how, and under what conditions learning contributes to more effective, efficient and engaged institutions. 

We have encouraging evidence that public professionals can gain knowledge and confidence around AI, including those starting with little familiarity. Now we need to follow what happens next as we work with governments to roll out new training that teaches people how to use AI in their specific role and to work in new ways. 

For government leaders, that means treating workforce learning as an investment whose results deserve sustained attention. This would include things like offering practical training, preparing managers to support it, giving employees opportunities to apply what they learn, and measuring what changes for the people they serve.

At a time when many people are skeptical of AI and uncertain about how it will benefit them, government has an opportunity to demonstrate what the technology can accomplish in the public interest. AI should not be a technology whose benefits accrue primarily to the companies building it or the people who can afford it. 

By equipping public servants with the skills to use AI responsibly and creatively, we can put powerful new capabilities in the hands of people whose job is to serve others. The measure of success will not be how many government employees use AI or how much time they save, but whether people experience better services, have more opportunities to participate in decisions, and see government solving problems that matter to them. Making those kinds of differences in people’s lives could make AI worthy of their trust and, more importantly, help rebuild their trust in government.

#31215. Kevin O’Leary has one rule for giving money to family. It works at any income

It’s pretty hard to feel bad for billionaires, but according to a host of incredibly wealthy people there’s one aspect of being filthy rich that is no fun at all: deciding what to do when friends and family ask you for money. 

How do those who can afford almost anything choose how to respond when someone they love comes looking for cash? Is there a way to balance a desire to help with a need to keep financial ties from spoiling your relationships? Shark Tank investor Kevin O’Leary recently talked about how billionaires think through these requests on a podcast. 

His words offer a peek into the hidden world of the super rich. But according to financial planners, they suggest a framework regular people can use to think through how and when to offer financial help to loved ones too. 

Being rich has one big downside 

Before we get into O’Leary’s comments, let me take a moment to try to convince you that the ability to cut a huge check to loved ones is actually a problem. Having nearly limitless resources has tremendous upsides, but several very wealthy people have spoken publicly about how being a potential piggy bank to everyone you meet can spoil relationships.

Bill Gates, for instance, told The New York Times how relieved he was that his daughter didn’t ask him for money when she was fundraising for her (now scandal-plagued) e-commerce startup. 

“I would have kept her on a short leash and be doing business reviews, which I would have found tricky, and I probably would have been overly nice but wondered if it was the right thing to do. Luckily, it never happened,” he said. 

He wasn’t the only Gates parent concerned that mixing money and family would end badly. His ex-wife, Melinda French Gates, also didn’t invest, stressing that financial handouts rob kids of learning opportunities. “She’s growing from this,” French Gates explained.

“It don’t work like that, man,” the rapper and all-around mogul insisted. “You gotta explain to him life isn’t like that.”

The loan would have killed the cousin’s hustle. It apparently also killed the good vibes between them. “You going home for solace, you want family, you’re going home for peace of mind,” Jay-Z said. 

Kevin O’Leary’s rule for giving away money 

VC Morgan Housel once summed up the problem of handing your loved ones (especially young loved ones) money this way: You either “ruin their ambition with inheritance, or risk some form of strife by denying them an easy life.”

On the Fifth Column podcast, O’Leary agreed. When asked what was the worst part of being a rich guy, the investor replied: “It’s your own family hitting on you for money, and it’s extremely difficult.” 

He continued, “Many people in your family will approach you and they will say, ‘I don’t want you to give me money. I want you to invest in my idea, and I want you to give me this for that, or I want to start a bowling alley or a restaurant or a business or whatever it is, and I’ve seen it all. In the beginning, you will feel the guilt of success and you will give them money, and you will slowly destroy your relationship with those people, and you will have horrible Thanksgivings.” 

That’s why O’Leary developed a rule to save his relationships (and his Thanksgivings). When someone comes to ask him for, say, $200,000 for whatever scheme or investment, he replies, “‘Look, I’m going to give you the $200,000. I don’t want to be paid back. It’s a gift, but I never ever want you ever to ask for money from me again, ever.’”

He explained, “I want to have Thanksgiving dinner and talk about family, not money. I’ve watched how bad money can screw up a family because [of] loans all over the place, you’re trying to get back money from your brother-in-law. That’s insane.” 

As Business Insider recently noted, O’Leary’s simple “one-time gift rule” has inspired other wealthy people to adopt a similar framework.

A lesson for non-billionaires, too 

Obviously, 99.9% of people reading this are not going to share this exact billionaire problem. But that doesn’t mean those of ordinary means have nothing to learn from the struggles of Kevin O’Leary and his fellow billionaires. 

Even if your investment in a loved one’s business is much more modest than a Bill Gates-size check, it still means you have to decide how much to get involved in day-to-day operations. The likely result will be strife. Hounding your brother-in-law to pay you back stresses your relationship whether the loan is for $200,000 or $200. 

That’s why at least some financial planners insist that flat gifts to assist with major life events, like contributing to tuition or a down payment, are often a wiser way to share your financial success with loved ones than a loan or investment. 

“If you’re a parent (or grandparent, or very kind aunt or cousin) who is fortunate enough to have the money to help your family, where should you start? Given the complexities and risks, I believe you should avoid funding a loved one’s business,” wealth planner Neil Torney advised in the Financial Times, for example.

So while you probably have fewer people hitting you up for lower amounts, you can still consider applying O’Leary’s one-and-done rule for financial assistance. Loans and investments with family are a minefield at any income level. A flat one-time gift might be a better bet. And if you can’t afford to make it a gift, maybe consider saving your Thanksgivings and make it a no.
